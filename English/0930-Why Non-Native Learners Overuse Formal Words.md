## 板块一：修正后的口语主题标题

### 英文标题

**Why Non-Native Learners Overuse Formal Words**

### 中文解析

原句 "Non-native learners tend to use more formal words (longer, complex ones) instead of the everyday words like 'do' or 'take' that native speakers use" 表达准确但过于冗长。修正后用 **overuse formal words** 简洁概括核心现象，标题像在聊一个英语学习者普遍存在的问题，而非学术论文标题。

## 板块四：5 个真实场景例句

### 例句 1（描述非母语者用词偏正式）

> **Well...** I dunno if you noticed, but non-native speakers always use these _big words_ (big words) that nobody actually says. Like, they'll say "I shall commence the task" instead of "I'm gonna _do_ (do) it." _Kinda_ (kinda) funny, honestly.

**连读提示**：kinda = kind of；gonna = going to；big words 中 /g/ 与 /w/ 连读

---

### 例句 2（让对方说简单点）

> **You know what?** Hey, can you _dumb it down_ (dumb it down) a little? You're using all these _complex_ (complex) words and I'm just... lost. Just tell me in _plain English_ (plain English), yeah?

**连读提示**：dumb it down 中 /m/ 与 /ɪ/ 连读；plain English 中 /n/ 与 /ɪ/ 连读

---

### 例句 3（描述说话太正式不自然）

> **I mean...** When he talks, he _sounds like a book_ (sounds like a book), you know? Every sentence is perfect, every word is formal. But it just doesn't _sound natural_ (sound natural). _Kinda_ (kinda) like talking to a robot.

**连读提示**：sounds like 中 /z/ 与 /laɪ/ 连读；kinda = kind of；sound natural 中 /d/ 闪音

---

### 例句 4（给出学习建议）

> **Honestly?** Stop trying to sound smart. _Native_ (native) speakers use _simple_ (simple) words—do, take, get, make. The trick is learning how to _use_ (use) those small words in a hundred different ways. _Wanna_ (wanna) sound more natural? Ditch the big words.

**连读提示**：wanna = want to；ditch the 中 the 弱读；native 中 /t/ 闪音

---

### 例句 5（总结看法）

> **Look...** At the end of the day, English isn't about using the fanciest words. It's about _getting your point across_ (getting your point across). _Cut to the chase_ (cut to the chase), keep it _simple_ (simple), and you'll sound way more _natural_ (natural) than any big-word user.

**连读提示**：getting your 中 /t/ 闪音；cut to 中 /t/ 不完全爆破；natural 中 /t/ 闪音

## 板块五：完整口语表达模板（约 5 分钟 / 650 词）

### 口语主题：Why Non-Native Learners Overuse Formal Words

**完整口语文稿：**

Hey, what's up everyone. So today I **wanna** talk about something I've noticed with a lot of English learners—**why non-native learners overuse formal words**. Yeah, I know, it sounds like a grammar nerd topic, but hear me out, 'cause this is actually one of the biggest things that makes you sound less natural when you speak English.

So, let me start with an example. I was talking to this guy the other day, right? He's from China, been studying English for like ten years. His grammar is perfect, his vocabulary is huge, he can talk about pretty much anything. But when he speaks, he says stuff like, "I shall **commence** the assignment **subsequently**." And I'm just standing there like... what? Nobody talks like that. Nobody.

A **native** speaker would just say, "I'm **gonna do** it later." That's it. Four words. Simple. **Natural**.

And this is super common, man. Non-native learners love their **big words**. They love words like "**commence**," "**subsequently**," "**utilize**," "**nevertheless**," "**furthermore**." Words that sound smart, words that look impressive on paper, words that... nobody actually says in real life.

Don't get me wrong—knowing these words is good. You need to understand them when you read them, you need to know what they mean. But **using** them in everyday conversation? That's a different story. 'Cause when you drop a **big word** in a casual chat, it doesn't make you sound smart. It makes you sound like you're reading from a textbook. It makes you sound **awkward**. It makes you sound like a **robot**.

So why do non-native learners do this? I think there are a few reasons.

**Reason one: they learned English from textbooks.** Textbooks are **formal**. They use **formal** language, **formal** grammar, **formal** vocabulary. 'Cause textbooks are designed to teach you "proper" English, not "real" English. So you learn that "**utilize**" is a word, and you think, "Oh, this is a good word, I should use it." But nobody tells you that in real life, people just say "**use**."

**Reason two: they think big words = smart.** A lot of learners think that if they use longer, more **complex** words, they'll sound more intelligent. And yeah, in writing, maybe. In an academic paper, sure. But in a casual conversation? No way. In fact, it's the opposite. Native speakers who use **big words** in everyday chat are often seen as pretentious, as trying too hard. Like, "Dude, just say what you mean, no need to show off your vocabulary."

**Reason three: they don't know the small words well enough.** This is the big one, man. The small words—**do**, **take**, **get**, **make**, **go**, **come**—these are the hardest words in English. Not because they're hard to pronounce, but because they have like a hundred different meanings and uses.

Take "**get**," for example. "I **get** it" means I understand. "I'm **getting** tired" means I'm becoming tired. "I **got** a package" means I received something. "**Get** out" means leave. "**Get** over it" means move on. "**Get** along" means have a good relationship. "**Get** away with it" means not get caught. All different meanings, all the same tiny word.

Non-native learners often skip these small words because they think they're "too easy." They want to learn the "impressive" words. But here's the truth—**native speakers live and die by these small words.** We use them all day, every day, in a hundred different ways. And if you don't know how to use them, you'll always sound like you're translating from your first language instead of actually speaking English.

So what's the fix? How do you stop overusing **formal** words and start sounding more **natural**?

**Tip one: listen to how native speakers actually talk.** Not the news, not TED talks, not academic lectures. Real people. Watch sitcoms, listen to podcasts, watch YouTube vloggers. Pay attention to the words they use. I promise you, they're not saying "**utilize**" or "**commence**." They're saying "**use**" and "**start**" and "**do**."

**Tip two: when you learn a new word, learn its casual alternative.** If you learn "**purchase**," also learn "**buy**." If you learn "**assist**," also learn "**help**." If you learn "**converse**," also learn "**talk**." And then—this is the important part—**use the casual one in conversation.** Save the formal one for writing.

**Tip three: practice using small words in different ways.** Pick one small word a week—like "**take**"—and learn all the different phrases: "**take** a break," "**take** a shower," "**take** off," "**take** out," "**take** over," "**take** after," "**take** it easy." Practice using them in sentences. The more you use these small words, the more natural you'll sound.

**Tip four: don't be afraid to sound "simple."** A lot of learners think simple words = simple mind. But that's not true. Some of the smartest people I know use the simplest words. 'Cause the goal of communication isn't to impress people with your vocabulary. The goal is to **get your point across**. To be understood. And simple words do that better than **big words** every single time.

Look, at the end of the day, English isn't a test. It's a tool. A tool for communication. And the best communicators aren't the ones who use the fanciest words. They're the ones who say what they mean, clearly and simply, in a way that people can understand.

So stop trying to sound like a textbook. Start trying to sound like a person. Ditch the **big words**. Embrace the small ones. **Keep it real**, keep it **simple**, and you'll be amazed at how much more **natural** you sound.

Alright, that's my take. Go **dumb it down** a little. Trust me, it's worth it. Catch you next time.
