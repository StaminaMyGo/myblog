## 5. Three Full-Length Model Responses

> **How to use these templates:** Read each aloud twice. First, focus on chunking — pause at every `/`. Second, emphasize the **bolded** syllables. Mimic natural academic speech, not robotic reading.

---

### Template 1 — Opinion Response (Agree/Disagree)

**Prompt:** *"Universities should ban generative AI in doctoral dissertation writing." Do you agree or disagree?*

**Model Response:**

> I **disagree** / that universities should impose a **total ban** / on generative AI / in doctoral writing.  
>  
> My first reason / is that a blanket prohibition / ignores the **legitimate** uses of AI / in literature synthesis / and language polishing. / Many non-native English-speaking researchers / use these tools / to **refine** clarity / without compromising **intellectual ownership**.  
>  
> Second, / universities should focus on **transparency** / and **accountability**, / not prohibition. / For example, / a PhD student could be required / to **disclose** which AI tools were used / and for what purpose. / This keeps a **human in the loop** / while still allowing **augmentation** of routine tasks.  
>  
> Of course, / I acknowledge / that AI-generated **fabrication** / is a serious threat. / But the solution / is **training** and **oversight**, / not an outright ban. / In the long run, / doctoral education should teach students / to use AI **ethically**, / because these tools / will only become more **pervasive**.  

**Key prosody notes:**  
- Pause before "but," "because," and "for example."  
- Stress "total ban," "legitimate," "transparency," "fabrication," "ethically."  
- Slightly lower pitch at the concession sentence: "Of course, I acknowledge…"

---

### Template 2 — Event Description (News/Incident)

**Prompt:** *Describe a recent event related to AI and academic integrity that illustrates the problem.*

**Model Response:**

> A **widely reported** incident / occurred in 2023 / at several U.S. universities / when AI-detection software / **flagged** a number of doctoral applicants' writing samples / as likely machine-generated.  
>  
> The problem / was that many of these applicants / were **non-native English speakers**, / and their formal, cautious writing style / triggered **false positives**. / According to a report / in **The Chronicle of Higher Education**, / some programs initially **rejected** otherwise strong candidates / before manual review **overturned** the decisions.  
>  
> This event / illustrates a broader tension / between **academic integrity** / and **algorithmic bias**. / It shows that detection tools / are not yet **reliable** enough / to serve as the sole judge / of student authorship. / The incident also pushed many universities / to issue **interim policies** / on AI use in admissions / and to stress / the importance of **human judgment** in disciplinary procedures.  

**Key prosody notes:**  
- Break after "The problem was" to signal contrast.  
- Stress "non-native," "false positives," "reliable," "human judgment."  
- Use a slightly faster pace for the chronological part of the event, then slow down for the implication.

---

### Template 3 — Implication Analysis (Cause–Effect / Significance)

**Prompt:** *Analyze the implications of AI-generated literature reviews for doctoral research quality.*

**Model Response:**

> The **widespread** use / of AI-generated literature reviews / has **profound implications** / for doctoral research quality.  
>  
> On the one hand, / AI can **expedite** the identification / of relevant sources / and reduce the **cognitive load** / of early-stage reading. / This may allow doctoral students / to spend more time / on **conceptual design** / and **empirical rigor**.  
>  
> On the other hand, / there is a real risk / of **subtle distortion**. / AI models sometimes **conflate** findings, / omit methodological limitations, / or generate **plausible but nonexistent** citations. / If students rely on these outputs / without **verifying** provenance, / the foundation of their research / becomes **compromised**.  
>  
> Therefore, / the key implication / is that doctoral training / must now include **AI literacy** / as a core competency. / Students need to learn / not how to avoid AI, / but how to **interrogate** its outputs, / trace sources, / and maintain **intellectual authority** over their own work. / Otherwise, / efficiency gains / will come at the cost / of **epistemic integrity**.  

**Key prosody notes:**  
- In "On the one hand… On the other hand…," raise pitch slightly to mark contrast.  
- Stress "profound," "conflate," "plausible but nonexistent," "epistemic integrity."  
- Slow down for the final sentence — it carries the thesis.

## 6. Divergent Thinking Angles

Use these prompts to extend your spoken responses beyond the standard template.

### 6.1 Related Historical & Current Events

- **The Reproducibility Project (2015–2016, Psychology)** — Showed many published findings could not be replicated; AI-generated summaries may worsen this by hiding methodological nuance.  
- **Nature's 2023 survey of researchers** — Found that a significant minority had used AI for manuscript preparation, but most worried about **fabrication** and **bias**.  
- **Stanford AI Index Report 2024** — Documented rapid adoption of generative AI in higher education and a parallel rise in institutional policy-making.  
- **MIT News study on ChatGPT and writing productivity** — Found AI reduced time on routine writing tasks but also lowered perceived ownership for some users.  
- **Harvard Graduate School of Education guidance** — Emphasized **transparency**, **instructor-student dialogue**, and **disclosure** over prohibition.  

### 6.2 Extended Discussion Angles

1. **Epistemological angle:** Does AI change what counts as "original contribution" in a dissertation?  
2. **Equity angle:** Do AI tools help level the playing field for non-native English speakers, or do detection systems penalize them disproportionately?  
3. **Labor market angle:** If AI can draft literature reviews, what doctoral skills remain valuable to employers?  
4. **Environmental angle:** Should researchers consider the carbon footprint of large language models when choosing tools?  
5. **Publication ethics angle:** How should journals verify whether a submission was meaningfully authored by a human?  
6. **Historical analogy angle:** How is resistance to AI similar to earlier resistance to calculators, statistical software, or internet search engines in academia?

## 7. Daily Practice Protocol

1. **Shadowing (5 minutes):** Read the warm-up quote and Template 1 aloud, recording yourself.  
2. **Lexical activation (5 minutes):** Choose five words and three phrases from the lists. Create original spoken sentences about your own field of study.  
3. **Extended response (10 minutes):** Answer one of the prompts from Section 6.2 aloud. Aim for 45–60 seconds. Focus on **chunking** and **stress**, not speed.  
4. **Self-assessment (3 minutes):** Listen to your recording. Check for:  
   - Did you pause at natural syntactic boundaries?  
   - Did you stress key academic nouns and contrasts?  
   - Did you avoid filler words like *"you know"* and *"like"*?  
5. **Vocabulary consolidation (2 minutes):** Write one sentence using a new word or phrase from today's card. Say it aloud three times with varied intonation.
